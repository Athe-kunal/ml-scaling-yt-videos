# Build toward four bit training

The most useful path for your video is **number formats → quantization error → neural network training → mixed precision → block scaling → FP4 training recipes**. The two posts you chose belong near the end: [Radical Numerics](https://www.radicalnumerics.ai/blog/nvfp4-part1) explains the NVFP4 numerical machinery, while [Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/end-to-end-4-bit-training/) shows a selective low-precision recipe used across a real training pipeline. Keep a crucial distinction visible throughout: **quantization-aware training (QAT), low-bit optimizer states, QLoRA, and low-precision training compute solve different problems**. “Four-bit training” does not necessarily mean every tensor, accumulation, and operation uses four bits. The guide below is ordered as a curriculum, with what to extract from each resource and how it can serve the video.

## Start with numerical formats and quantization error

A neural network represents its weights, activations, gradients, and optimizer states as finite sets of numbers. Teach the difference between **range** (how large and small the represented values can be) and **precision** (how closely spaced those values are). Integer representations have a uniform grid; floating-point spacing varies with magnitude. Reducing bits makes storage and data movement cheaper and can enable faster hardware operations, but also introduces rounding, clipping, underflow, and accumulated error. Hardware throughput is a property of the hardware and kernel, not a guarantee of equivalent end-to-end speedup.

A simple first diagram is the affine quantization equation **r ≈ s(q − z)**: `r` is the real value, `q` the integer code, `s` a scale, and `z` a zero point. Put several real values on a line and show their rounding to discrete grid points; then add an outlier and show how expanding the range makes the grid coarser for the bulk of values. Follow with symmetric versus asymmetric quantization and per-tensor, per-channel, and per-block scaling. The exact-zero motivation for a zero point is described in the [MLIR quantization documentation](https://llvm.googlesource.com/llvm-project/mlir/%2B/909d3b99f611c068636a3d328cc96b32f106999e/docs/Quantization.md).

## Training introduces gradients and more precision choices

Before studying FP4, draw one linear layer with **Y = XW**, then follow its two backward matrix multiplies: the activation gradient is proportional to `dY Wᵀ`, and the weight gradient to `Xᵀ dY`. Each product has different inputs and error sensitivity. Add separate boxes for the accumulator, master weights, optimizer states, and communications. This diagram gives a precise answer to “what is quantized?” for each paper. It also makes clear why low-precision training is harder than simply loading an already-trained weight tensor in a smaller format: rounding noise affects repeated updates, while gradients can have a different distribution and dynamic range from activations.

The historic bridge is [Mixed Precision Training](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.03740): FP16 computation with higher-precision accumulation and master weights, plus loss scaling to protect small gradients. Read the [NVIDIA mixed-precision tutorial](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/performance/mixed-precision-training/index.html) alongside it for worked explanations of underflow and loss scaling. Move to [FP8 Formats for Deep Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05433) for E4M3 and E5M2: additional exponent bits expand range at the expense of significand precision. The [FP8 primer](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/transformer-engine-releases/release-2.5/user-guide/examples/fp8_primer.html) adds scaling and practical examples. NVIDIA documents a hybrid recipe using E4M3 forward and E5M2 backward; treat that as a recipe, not a universal rule ([Transformer Engine documentation](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/transformer-engine/api/common.html)).

## Separate inference QAT, QLoRA, and low precision compute

These approaches overlap in vocabulary but have distinct goals. **Post-training quantization (PTQ)** takes an already trained model and produces a quantized model, usually for inference. **QAT** trains with simulated quantization so the resulting inference model tolerates it; the training operations are not automatically executed in low-bit arithmetic. The classic [Jacob et al. paper](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018/html/Jacob_Quantization_and_Training_CVPR_2018_paper.html) develops integer-only inference with training designed around quantization. The [PyTorch QAT blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/quantization-aware-training/) explains fake quantization and conversion; consult the [current TorchAO QAT documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/ao/stable/workflows/qat.html) for implementation APIs. This [QAT tutorial video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s3tqqBaRuHE) offers a hands-on example, though its APIs may have changed.

**QLoRA** fine-tunes trainable low-rank adapters while keeping a quantized base model frozen. It is useful context for four-bit *storage* and memory-efficient fine-tuning, but is not an example of full four-bit pretraining. The original [QLoRA paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314) explains NF4, double quantization, and paged optimizers. By contrast, the training recipes below target the numerical formats used by particular matrix multiplications during pretraining or later training stages. For every claim, ask which operands are quantized, which format accumulates the result, what remains higher precision, and whether the benefit is memory, compute, communication, or some combination.

## Microscaling makes FP4 practical, but recipes still differ

At four bits, the representable grid is extremely sparse. A scale shared across a large tensor can let a few outliers dictate the resolution for everyone else. **Microscaling** partitions values into small blocks with separate scales, improving local use of the available range at the cost of scale storage and implementation complexity. The [Microscaling Data Formats for Deep Learning paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10537) provides the conceptual bridge. The [Radical Numerics Part 1 article](https://www.radicalnumerics.ai/blog/nvfp4-part1) then develops floating-point formats, two-level scaling, outliers, stochastic rounding, Hadamard transforms, and the NVIDIA recipe with code. Its [Part 2](https://www.radicalnumerics.ai/blog/nvfp4-part2) traces the three training matrix multiplies into Transformer Engine and kernels.

**NVFP4 represents values using E2M1 elements, an E4M3 scale per block, and an FP32 tensor scale.** The common one-dimensional block is 16 values; NVIDIA also describes 16×16 scaling for weights. For a 16-value block, an eight-bit block scale gives an illustrative 4.5 bits per element before global-scale and other overhead—not exactly four bits of total representation per value. MXFP4 uses a different scaling design, including 32-element blocks and E8M0 scales. Consult [NVIDIA's NVFP4 recipe documentation](https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/transformer-engine/features/low_precision_training/nvfp4/nvfp4.html) and the [Radical Numerics explanation](https://www.radicalnumerics.ai/blog/nvfp4-part1) for the exact scheme.

The supporting techniques have distinct jobs. **Stochastic rounding** randomly chooses neighboring representable values according to their distance, reducing systematic rounding bias in suitable settings. A randomized **Hadamard transform** spreads concentrated outliers across coordinates before quantization; the recipe must preserve or account for the transform in the computation. Two-dimensional weight scaling helps manage transposed access in forward and backward products. These are recipe-specific numerical tools, not properties inherent to every four-bit format. The primary [Pretraining Large Language Models with NVFP4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25149) report explains their use and evaluation. This [NVFP4 paper explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IGn04ARFoSY) and NVIDIA's [reduced-precision technical talk](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/on-demand/session/gtc26-dlit81567/) are supplementary watches; the latter may require registration. NVIDIA's [What Is NVFP4?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UTfg-_EGurw) is accessible but focused on inference. Video content and duration have not been independently reviewed in full.

The [Thinking Machines article](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/end-to-end-4-bit-training/) is the production case study, and its title needs careful unpacking. It uses **NVFP4 for expert-layer forward passes, MXFP8 for backward passes, and higher precision for selected operations**; its “end-to-end” framing describes using a consistent numerical configuration across stages from pretraining through reinforcement learning, rather than four-bit execution for every operation. It also discusses quantized communication. The authors report **8% pretraining and 20% RL wall-clock gains** on their largest jobs relative to their full-MXFP8 baseline. These are results for their specific setup, not universal speedups or the same thing as peak matrix-multiply throughput.

For further research, [Four Over Six](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02010) studies adaptive block scaling and the uneven FP4 grid; [FP4 All the Way](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19115) explores a different recipe using predominantly FP4 weights, activations and gradients; [Quartet II](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22813) investigates gradient estimation; and [Normalized Architectures are Natively 4-Bit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06067) explores architecture co-design. Read these after the baseline recipe and check each paper's actual operation coverage, precision exceptions and experimental setup before comparing claims.

## Structure the video around a running example

Start with a handful of decimal weights and show how a narrow integer grid approximates them. Add a large outlier, show the loss of resolution, and then split the values into two blocks with separate scales. This one example gives a visual thread through clipping, rounding, scale selection, and microscaling. Introduce floating-point formats after the basic quantizer, using a number line rather than bit fields alone; bit fields then explain why E2M1 has such a sparse, nonuniform grid.

Next, build the training diagram from forward pass to loss, backward pass, gradient accumulation and optimizer update. Label each tensor and operation with its possible dtype, and use the diagram to contrast QAT, QLoRA, 8-bit optimizer states and actual low-precision matrix multiplication. Transition through FP16 and FP8 to explain why loss scaling, higher-precision accumulation and selective precision were introduced. Finally, revisit the outlier example to motivate block scaling, Hadamard transforms and stochastic rounding, then use NVIDIA's recipe and the Thinking Machines deployment as two concrete case studies. A final benchmark slide should specify **model, hardware, baseline, training stage, measured metric, and which operations used which precision**.

For an efficient first pass, watch the two MIT lectures, work through the PyTorch quantization blog, read the mixed-precision paper and FP8 primer, then read Radical Numerics Parts 1 and 2, the NVIDIA NVFP4 paper and documentation, and Thinking Machines. Return to the survey and advanced papers when building deeper sections or checking a particular claim. This order keeps the mathematical ideas grounded in a single question: how much numerical error can each part of training tolerate while making the whole system faster or smaller?
